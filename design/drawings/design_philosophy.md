# Membrane Cartography

A visual philosophy for documents that measure something fragile.

**Space and form.** The page is a calm surveying table. Large fields of warm, unprinted paper hold a small number of exact shapes, and the shapes are allowed to breathe. Forms are drawn rather than filled: a hairline contour, a second contour offset by a precise interval, a single straight stroke where structure lives. Curves come from measured control points, never from a free hand. The work is meticulously crafted: every line is the outcome of a calculation, and it reads like it was redrawn until nothing could be removed.

**Colour and material.** The palette is ink and membrane. A near‑black, slightly warm ink carries all structure. One translucent amber, in two strengths, stands for the skin stretched between the lines. A single signal red is reserved for the one line that changes the shape (a fold, a hinge, a decision). Everything else is the paper itself. Colour is never decoration. Each tint marks a material state, and the restraint is the product of painstaking calibration.

**Scale and rhythm.** Measurement is rendered as texture. Fine tick ladders, 1mm and 10mm graduations, small registration crosses and quiet index numbers repeat along the edges like the margins of a navigator's chart. Up close they are instruments; from across the room they become a woven border that gives the whole sheet its pulse. Repetition is patient and exact, the kind of density that only master‑level execution sustains without noise.

**Composition and balance.** Compositions sit on an unseen grid and are often deliberately asymmetric: one heavy subject, one light column of data, generous negative space between them. Mirror symmetry appears only where the subject itself is mirrored, and even then a small offset or an annotation breaks the stillness. Nothing touches the margin, nothing overlaps; alignments are checked twice, the way a master draughtsman checks a title block before signing it.

**Hierarchy and type.** Text is sparse and quiet. A thin, wide technical face names things; a monospaced face states numbers; a humanist sans explains only when explanation cannot be drawn. Titles are small and confident, numbers are exact, labels sit beside the line they name instead of on top of it. The page should feel like a specimen sheet from an imaginary institute of flight: the product of deep expertise, careful, almost reverent, and impossible to mistake for something rushed.
